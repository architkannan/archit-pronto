This model maps what the training infrastructure needs to produce, and which levers Pronto controls to close it.

Assumed
All 12 centres are treated as equivalent. The model divides the system-level requirement equally across all centres. In reality, centres likely differ in physical capacity, catchment population, and operational maturity. A city-level breakdown would require internal data.
Assumed
Training cycles run continuously. The model assumes each centre can run cycles back-to-back without operational gaps between cohorts. Scheduling constraints, trainer availability, and facility capacity may reduce the practical maximum below the theoretical 7 training cycles per month.
Assumed
The ramp penalty applies to net-new (growth) professionals only, not replacements. Replacement professionals have prior experience in the role; their ramp profile is genuinely unknown but is assumed to be faster than a first-time hire. Applying the penalty only to the growth cohort produces a conservative lower bound on the adjustment. If replacements also ramp slowly, the true adjusted requirement is higher. The default ramp assumption (3 weeks, 40% productivity: approximately 2-3 bookings/day) is a working estimate. Pronto's internal data on new-professional booking ramp curves should replace these defaults.
Assumed
The B2B supply channel is excluded. Pronto's agency-placed professional channel (announced April 2026) adds a parallel supply stream not modelled here. If agency placements contribute meaningfully to headcount, the gap narrows: but B2B candidates introduce a two-layer accountability structure that the quality gate must accommodate separately.
Scope
The ramp adjustment is a simplification. The model treats all new professionals as ramping identically: same duration, same productivity curve. In reality, ramp speed likely varies by prior experience, micromarket density, and hub manager quality. A cohort-level ramp model would require internal booking data by professional tenure. The adjustment shown here understates variance: the real range is wider than a single slider implies.
Scope
This model measures throughput, not quality. It counts the number of professionals who can be trained and activated: not whether they meet Pronto's quality standard. A higher pass rate assumption does not mean quality is maintained; it means the model assumes quality is maintained. The screening rubric and training standardisation questions sit outside this model.
Scope
Demand growth is treated as linear. The 70K/day target is modelled as a uniform ramp from 20K today. Actual demand may grow non-linearly: faster in existing dense markets, slower in new cities: which would change the micromarket-level supply requirement even if the system-level total is correct.
Scope
Retention is modelled as constant. The 30% monthly churn rate is applied uniformly across the full professional base. In practice, churn is likely higher among recently trained professionals and lower among tenured ones. A cohort-based retention model would produce a more precise replacement requirement but requires internal data.
Data
Three supply-side inputs are unknown from outside. Cohort size per centre, training cycles per month, and pass rate are Pronto's internal figures. The model's gap output is only as accurate as those inputs. The defaults used here are: 20 professionals per cohort (a mid-range estimate for a centre with one or two training rooms), and a 90% pass rate (the minimum consistent with the stated quality standard: if quality is the gate, fewer than 1 in 10 candidates should fail screening, not 1 in 4). These are working illustrations, not estimates of Pronto's actual state. Replace them with internal figures for a precise output.
Data
The 3-month window may already be shorter. The Feb 2026 baseline and June 2026 target imply a 4-month window: but professional activation, market seeding, and demand development all require lead time within that window. The effective training window available may be 10-12 weeks, not 16.

Four levers determine whether the gap closes.

They are not equal: they differ in how fast they can be pulled, how much they cost, and how certain the outcome is. Work through them in this order.

The model is designed to be used in this sequence:

  • Levers A and B are governance calls that require no new budget and can be decided in a room.
  • Lever D requires Pronto's internal ramp data to validate: the slider is a sensitivity test, not a recommendation.
  • Lever C is the last resort, not the first response.
